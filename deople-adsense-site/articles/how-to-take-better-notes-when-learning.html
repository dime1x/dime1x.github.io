How to Take Better Notes When You Are Learning Something New

Good notes are not a transcript of everything you hear or read. They are a compact record of the ideas, questions, and examples that help you understand a subject and use it later.

Published 2026-11-08 · 7 min read · Deople Editorial Team

Decide what the notes are for

Before starting, identify whether you need to remember key facts, follow a procedure, prepare for a test, or solve a practical problem. The purpose determines what deserves space. A tutorial may call for numbered steps; a conceptual topic may benefit from definitions and diagrams.

Capture ideas in your own words

Write the main point rather than copying every sentence. Paraphrasing forces you to check whether you understand the idea. Keep technical terms when precision matters, and add a short explanation or example beside unfamiliar vocabulary.

Use a consistent structure

Try a simple layout: topic, main idea, supporting details, example, and questions. Headings make notes easier to scan. For a process, use numbered steps. For comparisons, use a small table. Avoid adding decorative formatting that makes writing slower without improving meaning.

Add questions and connections

Mark anything unclear and turn it into a question to investigate. Connect new ideas to something you already know. A concrete example can reveal the limits of a definition and make an abstract concept easier to remember.

Review by recalling, not just rereading

Close the source and explain the main ideas from memory. Then check what you missed. Short review sessions spaced over several days can help you notice gaps before they become difficult to fix. Convert important points into questions if you use flashcards.

Keep notes useful over time

Add the source title and date when information may change. Correct errors when you discover them, and remove duplicate notes that no longer help. For fast-changing topics, record when you last checked the information.

Key takeaway

Useful notes are selective, written in your own words, organized around a purpose, and reviewed through active recall.
